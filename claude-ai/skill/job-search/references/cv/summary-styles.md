# Summary styles

The summary is the one part of the CV written for the reader identified in `analysis.md` Part 2. It is framing, so it may use broader language than the bullets, but every claim in it must be backed by a bullet below.

## 1. Opener by target level

The first words position the candidate at a level. Match the role applied for, inside an honesty bar.

| Role applied for | Opener | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Same level as a title the candidate has held (e.g. Senior) | "Senior <Function> Manager" | Matches a real title; nobody can ask "where were you that?" |
| Lead, when the JD literally uses "Lead" | "Lead <Function> Manager" | Mirrors the JD and signals scope match. Only here: "Lead" is a specific title and using it broadly invites the awkward question. |
| Staff, Principal, Director, VP, Head of | "<Function> Leader" | Describes function and seniority without claiming a title not held. Safe across every level above the candidate's current one. |
| Any level | Not "<Function> Manager" alone | Too neutral for senior roles; it under-positions. |

Check `profile/preferences.md` for the current title and the target titles before choosing. If the candidate's current title is below the target by two levels or more, "<Function> Leader" plus strong proof points is the honest ceiling; do not invent a title.

## 2. Two styles

**Investigate-then-build** (startups, scaleups, builder cultures). Show the reasoning, then the result:

- Not "Reduced custom engineering work by 90%". Instead: "Found that most engineering time went on one-off requests, then designed and shipped the self-serve platform that removed them."
- Inline proof points instead of adjectives: "Built a data platform from nothing by starting with one high-impact bet, validating it, then expanding it into the foundation the company ran on."
- If AI tooling is part of how the candidate works and the role values it, describe it as a working method, not a trophy: "Uses AI tools daily to move faster: prototypes ideas before committing engineering, builds internal tools, tests hypotheses in hours."
- Plain verbs, concrete nouns, short sentences.

**Strategic operator** (enterprise, chief-of-staff, roles reporting to executives). Lead with scope and judgement:

- Scale of what they ran (teams, customers, budgets, regions), the decisions they owned, the executives and functions they aligned.
- Still concrete. "Aligned executive, engineering, and go-to-market leadership on one source of truth for usage metrics" beats "drove cross-functional alignment".
- Compliance, governance, and risk appear here when the sector is regulated.

Pick one style per CV. Mixing them reads as hedging.

## 3. Stakeholder surfacing

Hiring managers and recruiters read "cross-functional alignment" as filler. Name the stakeholder types and counts from `role-context.md`, in the vocabulary the JD uses:

| Role type | Stakeholder vocabulary to use |
|---|---|
| Internal platform or infrastructure | engineers, analysts, data scientists, internal teams who build on the platform |
| Enterprise B2B SaaS | customers, design partners, commercial leadership, implementation teams |
| Regulated (finance, insurance, health, energy) | executives, risk and compliance, business units, regulated partners, auditors |
| Early-stage startup | founders, design partners, first users |
| Consumer or growth | users, marketing, analytics, growth teams |

One sentence in the summary should make it impossible to miss that the candidate has handled demanding stakeholders directly, with evidence ("about 100 enterprise customers through a pricing transition", "executive, engineering, and go-to-market leadership"). A recruiter once read a strong CV and asked for more stakeholder evidence; the evidence existed, it had only been implied.

## 4. The role-specific close

End with one or two sentences only this company could receive: what in their situation (from `analysis.md` Part 1, questions 7 and 8) the candidate's experience speaks to, and what they want to do there. Not "excited to join a mission-driven team".

## 5. What never goes in the summary

- Self-labels ("hands-on builder", "strategic operator", "AI-forward").
- Self-declarations of technical comfort ("comfortable with SQL", "technically proficient"). The bullets show it.
- "Proven track record", "cross-functional leadership", "stakeholder management", "strategic vision" as bare phrases.
- Scene-setting openers ("In today's data-driven world").
- Employer-specific AI or tooling work the candidate did on the job, unless the role is about exactly that. Those details live in the bullets, where they are evidence, not framing. Personal side projects may appear in the summary only when they are directly relevant to the role.
- Anything from the `role-context.md` interview-only list.
